### QB-DL4H-0001 · [OXFORD] · complete the sentence · L2 p.2 · first asked 2026-09-17
Q: "Training a neural network means minimising a loss over the data. The formal name for this principle is ______."
A: **Empirical risk minimisation** — minimising expected loss under the empirical data distribution, standing in for the true risk. (Maximum likelihood is related but narrower: it is what ERM reduces to for particular loss/noise pairings, e.g. cross-entropy.)

### QB-DL4H-0002 · [OXFORD] · open · L2 p.15–16 · first asked 2026-09-17
Q: Every loss encodes a noise model. Which noise does L2 assume, which does L1 assume, and why does MSE let outliers dominate?
A: L2 ↔ **Gaussian** noise; L1 ↔ **Laplace** noise; cross-entropy ↔ **categorical uncertainty**. "Choosing a loss is choosing which errors are plausible." MSE squares the residual, so a point 10× further out contributes 100× the loss and dominates the gradient — Gaussian tails are thin, so the loss treats a large residual as near-impossible and strains to fix it.

### QB-DL4H-0003 · [OXFORD] · structural · L2 p.22 · first asked 2026-09-17
Q: Dice = 2TP / (2TP + FP + FN). For a scan with many false positives, which of precision and recall is damaged, and which is untouched?
A: Precision is damaged (FP is in its denominator: TP/(TP+FP)). Recall is untouched — FP appears nowhere in TP/(TP+FN); only false negatives move recall. Dice is hurt by both, which is why it behaves like a balance of the two.

### QB-DL4H-0004 · [OXFORD] · open · L2 p.20 · first asked 2026-09-17
Q: Why were overlap-based losses like Dice proposed for segmentation instead of plain pixel-wise cross-entropy?
A: **Class imbalance.** Pixel-wise CE is summed over every pixel, and in a large scan the background vastly outnumbers a small structure — so the total is dominated by the sheer *count* of easy background pixels (each contributing a small loss), and predicting "all background" already scores well. Overlap losses measure agreement on the structure itself, so a small tumour still carries weight.
Asked: 2026-09-17 🟡 (core insight correct — small tumour drowned out — but said CE is "high for all the parts that match"; CE is *low* where prediction matches, it is their number that dominates)
Linked flag: FP-5

### QB-DL4H-0005 · [OXFORD] · pre-test → taught · L1 · asked 2026-09-18

Q: Regression targets contain large outliers. Which loss treats a big residual as nearly impossible and contorts the fit to chase it — L1 or L2? And which noise distribution is each assuming?

### QB-DL4H-0007 · [OXFORD] · reverse flashcard (FP-7 drill) · L2 p.2 · asked 2026-09-18
Q: Name the principle: minimising the average loss over your training sample as a stand-in for the loss you actually care about. What are you replacing with what?
A: **Empirical risk minimisation.** The substitution: the **true risk** — the expected loss over the real data distribution, which you cannot compute because you never see the distribution — is replaced by the **empirical risk**, the average loss over the finite sample you happen to hold. The whole edifice rests on that sample resembling what the model will meet in deployment; the deck flags this as the lecture's "implicit assumptions", and in healthcare it is where distribution shift between hospitals bites.
Asked: 2026-09-18 🟡 (named correctly — the same item he answered "MLE" to on D1, so the FP-7 substitution habit is fixed — but the substitution itself not articulated)

### QB-DL4H-0008 · [OXFORD] · structural formula-reading · PS1 Q1(b) · not yet asked
Q: The sigmoid derivative is $\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)\,(1-\sigma(z))$, and it peaks at $z=0$ where $\sigma=0.5$. Backpropagating through $L$ sigmoid layers multiplies one such factor per layer. **Point at what makes depth fatal here** — and say in one line why ReLU does not have the same problem.
A: The peak value is $0.5\times0.5=\mathbf{0.25}$ — so **every** layer multiplies the gradient by *at most* a quarter, never more. Through $L$ layers the gradient carries $(0.25)^L$; at $L=10$ that is $\approx10^{-6}$. The killer is that the factor is bounded **below 1**, so the shrinkage compounds and early layers stop learning — the vanishing gradient problem. ReLU's derivative is exactly **1** for $z>0$, so active neurons pass the gradient through unattenuated: nothing compounds, and depth stops being a penalty. That is what made very deep nets (ResNets) trainable.

### QB-DL4H-0009 · [OXFORD] · mechanism-only (FP-9 drill) · PS1 Q1(c) · not yet asked
Q: A neuron sits at $z_i=-2.5$. Its gradient is $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w_i}=\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial h_i}\cdot\dfrac{\partial h_i}{\partial z_i}\cdot\dfrac{\partial z_i}{\partial w_i}$. **Which factor is zero, and why can no amount of upstream error rescue it?**
A: The **middle** factor. $h_i=\mathrm{ReLU}(-2.5)=0$ and ReLU's derivative is $0$ for $z<0$, so $\partial h_i/\partial z_i=0$. It sits in a **product**, so a zero there annihilates the whole thing no matter how large $\partial L/\partial h_i$ is — the upstream error cannot reach the weight. If $z_i$ stays negative for every training example the weight never updates and the neuron is permanently **dead**. Leaky ReLU fixes it by making that middle factor $\alpha>0$ instead of $0$, so the product is never forced to zero.

### QB-DL4H-0010 · [OXFORD] · forced-choice direction (FP-5 drill) · PS1 Q2(b)(c) · not yet asked
Q: A loss has Hessian $H=\mathrm{diag}(1,20)$, so $\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}=20$. Stability needs $\alpha<2/\lambda_i$ in every direction. **Which eigenvalue sets the ceiling on the learning rate, and which direction is then left crawling?**
A: The **largest**, $\lambda_2=20$, sets the ceiling: $\alpha<2/20=0.1$. It binds because the steep direction diverges first. But that same $\alpha$ has to serve the flat direction too, where the effective step is $\alpha\lambda_1=0.1\times1=0.1$ — tiny. So the steep direction dictates the rate and the **flat** direction ($w_1$) crawls along the valley floor. One knob, two incompatible demands: that is what an ill-conditioned loss costs you.

### QB-DL4H-0011 · [OXFORD] · compare + mechanism · PS1 Q2(d)(e) · not yet asked
Q: Momentum and Adam both help on the loss above. **What does each actually do to the two directions, and what is the one difference that matters?**
A: **Momentum** averages past gradients. In the steep direction the gradients keep flipping sign as you bounce across the valley, so averaging **cancels** them and damps the oscillation; in the flat direction they all point the same way, so averaging **reinforces** them. It is a low-pass filter on the gradient. **Adam** accumulates squared gradients $v_i$ and divides the step by $\sqrt{v_i}$: large-gradient directions get scaled **down**, small-gradient directions **up**. The difference: Adam gives every coordinate its **own** learning rate, attacking the conditioning directly; momentum only smooths over time and never compensates for the curvature mismatch.

### QB-DL4H-0012 · [OXFORD] · structural formula-reading · PS1 Q5(b) · not yet asked
Q: Batch normalisation computes $\hat z_i=\dfrac{z_i-\mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2+\epsilon}}$. A model is run on **one** test example. **Read the numerator and say what every activation becomes** — then what that does to the model's output.
A: With a batch of one, $\mu_B=z_i$ — the mean *is* the value. So the numerator is $z_i-z_i=\mathbf{0}$, and $\hat z_i=0/\sqrt{\epsilon}=0$ for **every** activation, whatever the input. All information is destroyed and the model returns the **same prediction for any input**. The fix is the running (population) statistics gathered over training, which normalise relative to the training distribution and preserve differences between activations — which is why a model must be switched to **eval mode** at inference.

### QB-DL4H-0013 · [OXFORD] · interview-style, clinical deployment · PS1 Q5(a) · not yet asked
Q: A model trained at Hospital A is deployed at Hospital B and degrades, though the clinical task is identical. **What exactly is stored inside batch normalisation that breaks** — and name the architectural fix that removes the dependency altogether. Follow-up: why does that fix also survive a batch size of 2?
A: BatchNorm stores **running $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ estimated from Hospital A's data**, and uses those fixed numbers at inference. Different demographics, scanners or case mix shift Hospital B's activation distribution, so A's statistics normalise it into the **wrong range** and every downstream layer receives out-of-distribution input. Mitigations: train multi-site; recompute the running statistics on a calibration set from B. **Architectural fix: Group Normalisation** (or Layer Norm) — it normalises within channel groups **per example**, so there are no cross-batch statistics to carry over and nothing about Hospital A is baked in. Follow-up: because the statistics never involve the batch dimension at all, batch size is irrelevant — it behaves identically at size 2 or 200, and identically at train and test time. That is why it is standard in medical imaging (nnU-Net), where 3D volumes force tiny batches.

### QB-DL4H-0014 · [OXFORD] · formula-reading · PS2 Q2(a)(i) · not yet asked
Q: Dropout zeroes each neuron with probability $p$. During training the expected activation is
$$\mathbb{E}[\text{output}] = (1-p)\cdot a$$
At test time dropout is switched off, so every neuron fires and the output is $a$. **Point at what is now wrong, and name the factor that fixes it.** Follow-up: with $p=0.5$, is the test-time activation too large or too small, and by what factor?
A: The two expectations **do not match** — training produces $(1-p)a$, testing produces $a$, so every downstream layer suddenly receives inputs of a different magnitude than the ones its weights were fitted to, and the mismatch compounds layer by layer. Fix: scale the surviving activations by $\mathbf{1/(1-p)}$, restoring the expectation to $\frac{1}{1-p}\cdot(1-p)a = a$. Follow-up: **too large, by 2×** ($1/(1-0.5) = 2$). In practice this is done as **inverted dropout** — scale by $1/(1-p)$ during *training* so that test time needs no modification at all.
Why this format: the 09-23 finding — show the expression, get the mechanism. This is the same magnitude-domination reasoning that cracked FP-1.
Linked flag: FP-9 (mechanism), FP-1 (magnitude reasoning)

### QB-DL4H-0015 · [OXFORD] · forced choice · PS2 Q2(b)(ii) · not yet asked
Q: Two fundus images both come back with **mean predicted probability 0.72** for class 2 after 50 MC-dropout passes. Image 1 has standard deviation **0.04**; image 2 has **0.31**. One is cleared automatically, one goes to the ophthalmologist. **Which, and what is the other pole doing?** Follow-up: what would a triage system that used only the mean do with these two?
A: **Image 2 (σ = 0.31) goes to the ophthalmologist.** Its 50 thinned subnetworks span roughly 0.09–1.0 — nearly the whole grade range — so the subnetworks **strongly disagree** and the model is effectively guessing; the 0.72 is an average over disagreement, not a belief. Image 1's passes sit in roughly 0.65–0.81: every subnetwork agrees, so the prediction is robust and safe to clear. Follow-up: a mean-only system would treat them **identically** and auto-clear both, which is precisely the overconfident-wrong-prediction failure that loses a referral. Causes of high spread: image quality, pathology under-represented in training, or a genuine borderline between grades.

### QB-DL4H-0016 · [OXFORD] · pairing + discriminating test · PS2 Q2(c) · not yet asked
Q: Label each as **aleatoric** or **epistemic**, then give the one experiment that distinguishes them: (a) test images from a hospital whose scanner characteristics never appeared in training; (b) fundus images with poor focus and lens artefacts obscuring the retina.
A: (a) **Epistemic** — uncertainty about the *model*, arising from a region of input space it was never shown. (b) **Aleatoric** — uncertainty in the *data*: the information needed is not present in the image, so no model can recover it. **The discriminating test: add more training data of that kind.** If uncertainty **falls**, it was epistemic (the model lacked knowledge). If it **stays high**, it is aleatoric (the signal is genuinely absent and no amount of data helps). The implications diverge sharply: epistemic → collect multi-site data, retrain; aleatoric → fix acquisition, reject and re-image. Real cases mix both — a new scanner also introduces noise characteristics, and poor images from under-represented groups carry an epistemic component too.
Linked flag: FP-6 (pairing with reason), FP-5

### QB-DL4H-0017 · [OXFORD] · formula-reading · PS2 Q1(c)(ii) · not yet asked
Q: An STN's localisation network emits 6 numbers forming the 2×3 affine matrix
$$\begin{pmatrix} a & b & t_x \\ c & d & t_y \end{pmatrix}$$
To restrict it to **rigid** motion (rotation + translation only) the top-left block is re-parameterised as
$$\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$$
**How many free parameters remain, and what do the constraints $a^2+c^2=1$ and $b = -c$ forbid?** Follow-up: why parameterise by the angle $\theta$ rather than letting the network output $a,b,c,d$ and penalising non-rigidity?
A: **Three** free parameters — $(\theta, t_x, t_y)$, down from six. $a^2+c^2=\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$ forbids **scaling** (the columns are unit length, so lengths are preserved); $b=-c$ with $a=d$ forbids **shear and reflection**, leaving the matrix orthogonal with determinant $+1$. Together they rule out everything except rotation, so anatomy cannot be stretched or squashed. Follow-up: because the constraint is then **structural rather than encouraged** — the network emits $(\theta, t_x, t_y)$ and a fixed layer builds $(\cos\theta, -\sin\theta, t_x, \sin\theta, \cos\theta, t_y)$, so no output the network can produce is non-rigid. A penalty is a soft preference the optimiser can trade away; a parameterisation is a guarantee. A single angle also has no gimbal-type singularity to fall into.
Why this format: structural, zero arithmetic — the answer is "count the free symbols and say what each constraint kills".
Linked flag: FP-9

### QB-DL4H-0018 · [OXFORD] · mechanism, two-task contrast · PS2 Q1(a) · not yet asked
Q: Convolution is translation **equivariant**: shift the input and the feature map shifts with it. Global average pooling takes the spatial mean of each feature map. **What property does GAP convert equivariance into, and why does that make it right for classifying cardiomegaly from a chest X-ray but wrong for segmenting lung nodules?**
A: GAP converts translation **equivariance into translation invariance**: averaging over all spatial positions returns the same scalar no matter where in the image the feature sits. For **cardiomegaly classification** that is exactly the desired behaviour — the answer is yes/no and must not depend on patient positioning or where in the frame the heart falls, so discarding position is a feature, not a loss. For **nodule segmentation** the output is a mask that must preserve spatial correspondence with the input: if the nodule shifts, the mask must shift too. GAP destroys the "where", retaining only "whether such a feature was detected anywhere" — so UNet-style architectures avoid it and use skip connections to carry spatial detail to the decoder instead. The general trade-off: GAP buys perfect translation invariance by throwing away all spatial information.
Linked flag: FP-9, FP-5 (equivariance vs invariance is a classic inversion pair)

### QB-DL4H-0019 · [OXFORD] · mechanism + counter-scenario · PS2 Q1(b) · not yet asked
Q: UNet concatenates encoder features onto decoder features at matching resolutions. **What assumption about input and output does that encode** — and name a medical imaging setting where the assumption fails. Follow-up: a student proposes dropping the skips and using a much deeper bottleneck to preserve spatial information instead. What does that do to the optimisation problem?
A: It encodes **spatial correspondence**: that output location $(i,j)$ is about input location $(i,j)$, with no geometric transformation between them, so encoder features at a position are directly useful to the decoder at that same position. **Violated** whenever the output is in a different frame from the input — registration between scans where the patient moved between acquisitions (voxel $(i,j,k)$ in one no longer corresponds to the other), cross-modality synthesis with resampling, or any task whose axes are permuted or reprojected, where encoder position $(i,j)$ has no direct decoder counterpart. Follow-up: the deeper bottleneck must now **learn** to reconstruct spatial detail that the skips hand over for free, so it needs capacity and data to recover information it was given architecturally before; and the gradient path from loss to early encoder layers grows longer with no shortcut, which is the vanishing-gradient and degradation problem skips were introduced to solve. Strictly harder optimisation for strictly less spatial fidelity.
Linked flag: FP-9

### QB-DL4H-0020 · [OXFORD] · mechanism, "why not" · PS2 Q2(a)(ii) · not yet asked
Q: Dropout is standard before fully-connected layers but rarely applied to **early convolutional** layers, where it can even hurt. **Why does the same technique fail there?** Follow-up: what variant is used instead, and what does it change?
A: Because early feature maps are **spatially redundant**. At high resolution adjacent pixels are highly correlated, so zeroing the activation at $(i,j)$ leaves its neighbours $(i\pm1, j\pm1)$ holding nearly identical information — the network simply fills the hole from next door, and the regularisation does nothing. In an FC layer each neuron encodes an independent feature, so dropping one genuinely removes unique information. Three further costs: early layers learn the edge and texture detectors **everything** downstream depends on, and randomly removing them destabilises their training and wastes capacity on redundant copies; and early layers already receive the weakest gradients, so added noise degrades an already faint signal. Follow-up: **spatial dropout** (drop an entire feature map) or **DropBlock** (drop contiguous regions) — both remove spatially *coherent* information, which cannot be reconstructed from neighbours, so the difficulty becomes desirable again.
Linked flag: FP-9

### QB-DL4H-0021 · [OXFORD] · interview-style, clinical trust · PS2 Q3 · not yet asked
Q: 500 labelled dermatoscopy images (200 pos / 300 neg) for a rare autoimmune skin condition. Three candidate source models: **ImageNet** (1.2M natural images), **Model P** (200k chest X-rays, pneumonia), **Model Q** (100k dermatoscopy images, melanoma). **Rank them and justify the ranking by what transfers.** Then: a clinician asks *"if the best model was originally trained to recognise dogs and cars, how can I trust it to diagnose skin lesions?"* — answer them. Follow-up: fine-tuning end-to-end at learning rate $10^{-2}$ makes validation accuracy fall sharply and then partly recover. Why?
A: **Q > ImageNet > P.** Q shares the target's imaging modality, magnification, illumination and lesion morphology, so both low- *and* high-level features transfer, and 500 images is enough to move a head. ImageNet is generic but enormously diverse: its early layers (edges, colour blobs, textures) are near-universal and do transfer, even though its object-level features do not. P is the trap — a medical dataset, which feels closer, but grayscale thoracic radiographs share almost no visual statistics with colour dermatoscopy, so only the most generic early filters survive; domain match matters more than the word "medical". **To the clinician:** the model is not reasoning about dogs. What transferred is the first few layers — edge, colour and texture detectors that are properties of *images*, not of dogs — while the layers that decide the diagnosis are retrained entirely on dermatoscopy and validated on held-out dermatoscopy. Trust rests on that held-out performance, not on the provenance of the initialisation. **Follow-up:** a learning rate of $10^{-2}$ is appropriate for a randomly initialised head but enormous for pretrained weights sitting near a good optimum. The first large steps **destroy** the pretrained features (catastrophic forgetting), so accuracy collapses; training then re-converges from the wreckage toward a worse optimum than it started from, which is the partial recovery. Remedies: much smaller LR on the backbone, discriminative per-layer rates, warmup, or strategy (C) — freeze, train the head, then progressively unfreeze.
Linked flag: FP-9

### QB-DL4H-0022 · [OXFORD] · formula-reading · PS3 Q4(c) · not yet asked
Q: An affine flow layer applies, elementwise, $y_i = \exp(s_i)\,x_i + t_i$. **Write the inverse**, then say **what the log-Jacobian determinant reduces to** and why that is the whole point of the layer. Follow-up: a single such layer touches each dimension independently — so why must many be composed, and what has to sit between them?
A: Inverse: $x_i = (y_i - t_i)\exp(-s_i)$ — invertible for **any** real $s_i$, because $\exp(s_i)$ is never zero, which is why the scale is parameterised as an exponential rather than directly. The Jacobian $\partial y_i/\partial x_j$ is $\exp(s_i)$ on the diagonal and **zero off it**, since $y_i$ depends only on $x_i$. A diagonal matrix's determinant is the product of its diagonal, so
$$\log|\det J_f| = \sum_{i=1}^{d} s_i$$
— a **sum of $d$ learned scalars, $O(d)$**, against $O(d^3)$ for a general $d\times d$ determinant. That tractability *is* the design: exact likelihood stays computable at image or ECG dimensionality. Follow-up: one layer can only rescale and shift each dimension, so it cannot model any *dependency* between them. Composing layers adds expressiveness, but only if **permutations (or invertible 1×1 convolutions) are interleaved** — otherwise dimension $i$ is only ever transformed by its own $s_i, t_i$ and the dimensions never mix.
Why this format: the 09-23 finding — show the expression, get the mechanism.
Linked flag: FP-9

### QB-DL4H-0023 · [OXFORD] · formula-reading · PS3 Q1(a)–(b) · not yet asked
Q: With the generator fixed, the optimal GAN discriminator is
$$D^*(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_G(x)}$$
**Read the value off this expression for the case where the generator has perfectly matched the data distribution**, and say what that number means about the discriminator. Follow-up: what is that state called, and what does the non-saturating generator loss change about getting there?
A: If $p_G = p_{\text{data}}$ then numerator and denominator give $p/(p+p) = \tfrac{1}{2}$ **everywhere**. A discriminator outputting ½ for every input is **guessing at chance** — it has no information distinguishing real from synthetic, which is exactly the state the generator was driving towards. That is the **Nash equilibrium** of the minimax game. Follow-up: the original generator loss is $\log(1 - D(G(z)))$, which is *flattest precisely when the generator is worst* — a discriminator confidently rejecting fakes gives almost no gradient, so a bad generator cannot escape. The non-saturating form maximises $\log D(G(z))$ instead, which is **steep when the generator is losing**, so early training actually moves. Same fixed point, usable gradients.
Linked flag: FP-9

### QB-DL4H-0024 · [OXFORD] · forced choice, directional · PS3 Q3(b)(ii) · not yet asked
Q: The gradient reversal layer in DANN is the **identity on the forward pass** and multiplies the gradient by $-\lambda I$ on the backward pass. **Which direction does that push the feature extractor — towards features that identify the hospital, or away from them? And what would the other sign be doing?** Follow-up: what does the GRL buy you over writing an alternating two-loop optimisation?
A: **Away.** The domain discriminator's gradient points towards features that *help* identify the hospital; negating it turns every such step into a step that makes features **less** domain-discriminative. The other sign would do the opposite — actively train the extractor to broadcast which hospital the scan came from, the precise failure you are trying to remove. $\lambda$ sets how hard that push is relative to the task loss. Follow-up: the reversal makes the minimax objective expressible in a **single forward–backward pass**, so the whole thing trains end-to-end with an ordinary optimiser. No alternating loop to balance, no inner/outer step-ratio to tune — the adversarial structure lives in one layer's backward rule instead of in the training schedule.
Linked flag: FP-5 (his best-performing format, and sign-direction is exactly the inversion trap)

### QB-DL4H-0025 · [OXFORD] · pairing with reason · PS3 Q1(c) · not yet asked
Q: Three standard GAN failure modes — **mode collapse**, **vanishing gradients**, **oscillation**. Give the mathematical cause of each and one mitigation. Follow-up: which of the three does the non-saturating loss address?
A: **Mode collapse** — the generator finds a small set of outputs the discriminator currently scores well and maps most latent codes there; nothing in the objective rewards *covering* $p_{\text{data}}$, only fooling $D$ pointwise. Mitigations: minibatch discrimination, unrolled GANs, or a Wasserstein objective. **Vanishing gradients** — when $D$ becomes too strong it saturates, $\log(1-D(G(z)))$ flattens, and $G$'s gradient goes to nearly zero just when it most needs signal. Mitigations: the non-saturating loss, or WGAN/WGAN-GP whose critic gives usable gradients everywhere. **Oscillation** — simultaneous gradient descent on a minimax objective is not descent on anything; the pair can **cycle around** the equilibrium rather than converge, because each player's best response keeps moving. Mitigations: two-timescale learning rates, spectral normalisation, averaging iterates. Follow-up: **vanishing gradients**, and only that one — it changes the gradient landscape, not the mode-coverage incentive or the dynamics of the game.
Linked flag: FP-9, FP-6

### QB-DL4H-0026 · [OXFORD] · forced choice, applied · PS3 Q2(b)(ii) · not yet asked
Q: SimCLR's default augmentations for natural images are **aggressive colour jitter**, **random cropping** and **greyscale conversion**. Go through each for **chest X-rays** and then for **histopathology slides**, and say which are fine and which destroy the signal. Follow-up: what is the general rule you are applying?
A: **Chest X-ray** — colour jitter is near-meaningless (the image is already single-channel intensity) and greyscale conversion is a **no-op**; intensity/contrast jitter is the useful analogue and is fine, since scanners vary that way anyway. Random cropping needs care: crop too aggressively and you remove the pathology or the anatomical context that makes a finding interpretable, so the positive pair may no longer share a label. **Histopathology** — colour jitter and greyscale are the opposite: **stain colour is the signal**. H&E hue distinguishes nuclei from cytoplasm, so greyscale throws away the diagnostic channel; mild stain-colour jitter is actually valuable because it mimics real inter-lab staining variation, but aggressive jitter crosses into destroying the feature. Random cropping is the *safest* one here, because the tissue is approximately stationary — any patch of a slide is still that slide's tissue. Follow-up: **an augmentation is valid only if it preserves the label** — it must model variation that genuinely occurs in acquisition, not variation that changes the diagnosis. Importing natural-image defaults unexamined is how a pretext task learns to ignore the one channel that mattered.
Linked flag: FP-5

### QB-DL4H-0027 · [OXFORD] · forced choice · PS3 Q2(c) · not yet asked
Q: After self-supervised pretraining you can evaluate by **linear probing** (freeze the backbone, train a linear classifier) or **fine-tuning** (update backbone weights). **You have 100 labelled examples. Which, and what goes wrong with the other?** Follow-up: what does each of the two actually *measure*?
A: **Linear probing.** With 100 labels, fine-tuning a backbone of millions of parameters has far more capacity than the data can constrain — it overfits, and worse, it **destroys the pretrained representation** that was the entire asset, with no validation set large enough to detect the damage. A linear head on frozen features has a handful of parameters per class and degrades gracefully. Follow-up: linear probing measures **how linearly separable the pretrained representation already is** — a property of the representation, which is why it is the standard SSL benchmark. Fine-tuning measures **the best achievable task performance given this initialisation**, which is what you want when labels are plentiful. Different questions, and the label budget decides which one you are allowed to ask. Middle ground when you have a few hundred: progressive unfreezing from the head down, with a much smaller learning rate on the backbone.
Linked flag: FP-5, FP-9

### QB-DL4H-0028 · [OXFORD] · interview-style, clinical deployment · PS3 Q3(d)(iii) · not yet asked
Q: Your pneumonia model is about to go live at Hospital B — different scanner, different population. **There is one intervention worth doing first that requires no retraining and no labels at all. What is it, why does it work, and roughly how much of the gap does it recover?** Follow-up: what does it *not* fix?
A: **Recompute the batch-normalisation running statistics on a small unlabelled calibration set from Hospital B**, keeping the learned $\gamma$ and $\beta$ fixed. It works because BN stores Hospital A's running $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ and applies those fixed numbers at inference; B's activation distribution differs, so A's statistics normalise it into the **wrong range** and the error compounds through every later layer. Recomputing them re-centres the features where the trained weights expect them. It takes minutes, needs no labels and no gradient steps, and empirically recovers **50–80%** of the domain-shift gap. Follow-up: it only corrects first- and second-order *marginal* feature statistics. It does nothing about a genuine change in the **conditional** $p(y\mid x)$ — different case mix, different disease prevalence, a pathology presenting differently in this population — nor about anatomy or protocol differences large enough to shift the decision boundary itself. It is the cheapest 80%, not a substitute for adaptation or local validation.
Cross-link: builds directly on QB-DL4H-0013 (BatchNorm domain shift) — ask them in sequence.

### QB-DL4H-0029 · [OXFORD] · formula-reading · PS3 Q2(b)(i) · not yet asked
Q: SimCLR's InfoNCE loss, for a positive pair $(i,j)$:
$$\mathcal{L} = -\log \frac{\exp\!\big(\text{sim}(z_i,z_j)/\tau\big)}{\sum_{k\neq i}\exp\!\big(\text{sim}(z_i,z_k)/\tau\big)}$$
**Point at the numerator and then the denominator and say what each is doing to the representation.** Follow-up: why does a large batch size matter so much, and what does $\tau$ control?
A: It is a **softmax classification over "which of these is my other view"**. The **numerator** pulls the two augmented views of the same image together — maximising it means increasing the similarity of the positive pair. The **denominator** sums over the positive *and all negatives*, so minimising the loss simultaneously pushes $z_i$ away from every other image in the batch. Together: **invariant to the augmentation, discriminative between instances** — the representation must keep whatever survives cropping and jitter, and discard whatever does not. Follow-up: each image gets only $B-1$ negatives, and the batch *is* the negative set, so batch size is the number of distinct things the representation is forced to tell itself apart from. SimCLR needed batches in the thousands for exactly this; too few negatives and a coarse representation suffices, which is why MoCo introduced a momentum queue and BYOL/SimSiam removed negatives altogether. $\tau$ is the **temperature**: small $\tau$ sharpens the distribution over negatives, so the loss is dominated by the hardest ones; large $\tau$ flattens it and treats all negatives alike.
Linked flag: FP-9
